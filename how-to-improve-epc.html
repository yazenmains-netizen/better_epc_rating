How to Improve Your EPC Rating

Which improvements deliver the most points, what they cost, the right order to do them — and the mistakes that cost landlords thousands.

How EPC ratings are calculated

EPC ratings are calculated using a methodology called RdSAP — Reduced Data Standard Assessment Procedure. An assessor visits your property, takes measurements and observations, and enters the data into software that models the property's energy performance.

The software outputs an energy efficiency score from 1 to 100. This score is then mapped to a rating band:

  • A: 92–100 (most efficient)
  • B: 81–91
  • C: 69–80
  • D: 55–68
  • E: 39–54
  • F: 21–38
  • G: 1–20 (least efficient)

The score is based on a standardised occupancy model — it reflects the theoretical energy performance of the building, not your actual bills. This is why two identical properties with very different tenants get the same EPC score.

What the assessor is looking at

Heating system

Main heating type and efficiency

The type, fuel, age, and efficiency of the boiler or heat source. One of the highest weighted factors in the calculation.

Insulation

Walls, roof and floor

Wall construction type (solid, cavity, timber frame), roof insulation depth, and floor insulation where applicable.

Windows

Glazing type and age

Single, double, or triple glazing. Age of the glazing also affects the assumed U-value (heat loss rate).

Hot water

Hot water cylinder and system

Whether a cylinder is insulated, the type of hot water heating, and any immersion heater usage assumptions.

Lighting

Low-energy lighting percentage

The proportion of fixed light fittings using LED or CFL bulbs. A minor but easy win.

Renewables

Solar panels, heat pumps

Any renewable energy generation on the property. Can significantly boost the score but only after fabric improvements are made.

The most impactful improvements

This table ranks common improvements by the typical score uplift they deliver, alongside estimated cost ranges. These are approximations — actual uplift depends on the property's current state and construction type.

Improvement Typical score uplift Typical cost Notes
Solid wall insulation (external) +15–25 points £8,000–20,000 Highest impact for older solid-wall properties. Often cost-prohibitive but may be grant-funded.
Solid wall insulation (internal) +12–20 points £4,500–12,000 Less disruptive than external. Loses some floor area. Good option for terraced properties.
Air source heat pump +8–18 points £8,000–14,000 Only effective if the property is already well insulated. Don't fit before sorting fabric.
Cavity wall insulation +4–10 points £400–1,200 One of the best value measures available. Check suitability first — not all cavities can be filled.
Loft insulation (to 270mm) +4–8 points £300–600 Very cost-effective if the loft is accessible and currently uninsulated or under-insulated.
New A-rated gas boiler +3–6 points £2,000–4,000 Replacing an old G-rated boiler. Not worth doing if the property lacks insulation — you'll heat the walls.
Solar photovoltaic panels (3.5kW) +5–10 points £4,000–8,000 Good long-term investment. Only do this after fabric improvements — otherwise you're generating energy for a leaky building.
Room thermostat and TRVs +2–4 points £200–600 Often overlooked. Cheap and counts toward the score if controls are currently basic.
Hot water cylinder insulation +1–3 points £15–50 An extremely cheap improvement if the cylinder is uninsulated. Takes minutes.
Draught proofing +1–2 points £50–200 Useful for older properties with gaps around windows, doors, and chimneys.
LED lighting throughout +1–2 points £100–300 Easy wins if the property still has significant non-LED lighting. Assessors count the proportion of low-energy fittings.

Important: Score uplifts are estimates based on typical properties. The actual impact on your specific property depends on its current condition, construction type, and what's already been done. A proper survey models this precisely — which is exactly why going straight to a contractor without one is a gamble.

The right order to make improvements

This is where most landlords go wrong. The order of improvements matters because each measure you install changes what the next one needs to do. Installing a heat pump before insulating, for example, means you'll need a larger — and more expensive — system than if you'd sorted the fabric first.

The correct approach follows a consistent principle: reduce heat loss before improving heat generation.

1
Fabric first — insulation and airtightness
Loft insulation, cavity wall or solid wall insulation, draught proofing, floor insulation where accessible. Reducing how quickly the building loses heat is the foundation for everything else. There is no point improving your heating system if the heat immediately escapes through the walls and roof.
2
Heating efficiency — boiler, controls, hot water
Once the fabric is addressed, heating improvements deliver their full effect. An A-rated boiler in an insulated property performs very differently from the same boiler in an uninsulated one. Heating controls — thermostat, TRVs, programmer — are often missed despite being cheap and impactful.
3
Glazing — windows and doors
Double glazing is often assumed to be a high-priority improvement, but it ranks below insulation and heating in terms of score uplift per pound spent. Upgrade if the glazing is single-pane or very old, but don't expect double glazing alone to move a D to a C.
4
Renewables — solar panels, heat pumps
Only after the above. Solar panels on a leaky building generate energy that largely heats the street. Heat pumps need a well-insulated property to work efficiently and to be correctly sized — a common and expensive mistake is fitting an oversized heat pump in an under-insulated property.

Mistakes that cost landlords money

1
Going straight to a contractor without a survey
Contractors get paid to install things. They are not incentivised to tell you that a simpler, cheaper measure would achieve the same result. Without independent advice, you're relying on a salesperson to tell you what you need — which is not the same as knowing what you need.
2
Fitting a heat pump before insulating
Heat pumps work by running at lower flow temperatures for longer periods. In an uninsulated property, they struggle to maintain temperature and run inefficiently. You may also end up with a system that's spec'd too large — and oversized heat pumps cycle on and off, which reduces their lifespan and efficiency.
3
Installing cavity wall insulation in an unsuitable property
Cavity wall insulation is one of the best-value measures available — but only if the cavity is suitable. Properties in exposed locations, with defective brickwork, or with cavities that are too narrow can suffer damp problems after incorrect installation. A survey checks suitability before you commit.
4
Replacing a boiler that doesn't need replacing
A boiler replacement delivers a modest score uplift (3–6 points). If your property is currently D-rated and you spend £3,000 on a new boiler but the property needed loft insulation and heating controls instead, you may have spent three times as much for the same result — or missed the C boundary entirely.
5
Assuming double glazing is the main priority
Double glazing is visible and tangible — so landlords often assume it's important for EPC. It helps, but it delivers a smaller uplift per pound than insulation or heating controls. In most cases, loft insulation delivers double the score improvement at a fraction of the cost.
6
Waiting until 2029
The closer you get to the 2030 deadline, the more expensive everything becomes. Surveyor availability tightens, contractor lead times extend, and prices rise as demand spikes. Landlords who act in 2024–2026 consistently pay less than those who act in 2028–2029 for the same works.
