What the law currently requires

The Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards (MEES), introduced under the Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015, set the legal floor for energy performance in private rented properties.

Since April 2020, landlords in England and Wales have been unable to grant a new tenancy for a property with an EPC rating below E. Since April 2023, this applies to all tenancies — including existing ones that were already running.

In practice: if your property is currently rated F or G, you cannot legally let it without an approved exemption. This is already in force. It is not a future deadline.

If your property is rated F or G: You are currently in breach of MEES regulations unless you hold a valid exemption. Contact us immediately — this is a live compliance issue, not a future one.

The 2028 and 2030 deadlines

The government has confirmed that the minimum EPC requirement for private rented properties will rise to C. The implementation is staged:

Every private rented property in England and Wales — including those with existing tenants — must be EPC C or above. No exceptions without a registered exemption.

The demand problem: Every landlord in England and Wales faces the same 2030 deadline. When a large proportion try to act at the same time, surveyor and contractor costs will rise significantly. Landlords who act early will pay less — both for the assessment and for the works.

The fines for non-compliance

Local authorities are responsible for enforcing MEES. The penalty regime for renting a non-compliant property is:

BreachMaximum penalty
Letting a property below the minimum EPC ratingUp to £30,000 per property
Providing false or misleading information to register an exemptionUp to £5,000
Failure to comply with a compliance noticeUp to £5,000

The penalties are per property, not per landlord. A landlord with five non-compliant properties could, in theory, face £150,000 in fines.

Under the Renters' Rights Act 2024, tenants also have significantly strengthened rights to challenge landlords on energy efficiency grounds. The risk for non-compliant landlords is both regulatory and legal.

The £10,000 cost cap

Landlords are not required to spend unlimited amounts to reach EPC C. A cost cap of £10,000 per property applies — from October 2025, any spending on qualifying energy efficiency improvements counts toward this cap.

If a landlord has spent £10,000 and the property still cannot reach EPC C, they can register a "high cost" exemption. The property must still reach the highest rating achievable within that spend.

Why the cost cap matters for your strategy: If you're planning works anyway — insulation, a new boiler, double glazing — record the spend carefully from October 2025 onwards. This expenditure counts toward your £10,000 cap and can support an exemption application if needed.

Who is exempt

Not all properties are required to comply. The following categories may be eligible for an exemption:

Property exemptions

  • Listed buildings — where compliance works would unacceptably alter the character or appearance of the building
  • Properties where consent cannot be obtained — if a tenant or superior landlord refuses consent for works, an exemption may be registered
  • Properties where the recommended works would devalue the property by more than 5% — must be supported by an independent surveyor's report
  • New tenancies of less than 6 months — short-term lets may fall outside the scope of MEES

High cost exemption

If the cheapest recommended improvements that would bring the property to EPC E (current minimum) cost more than £3,500, a landlord can register a high cost exemption. For the upcoming EPC C requirement, the cap is £10,000.

Exemptions are temporary: Most exemptions last for 5 years. After that, you must make another attempt to comply or register a new exemption. Exemptions must be registered on the PRS Exemptions Register — they are not automatic.

The RdSAP methodology change

There is a significant change coming in 2026–2027 that most landlords are unaware of: the Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP) used to calculate EPC ratings is being updated from RdSAP 10 to the Home Energy Model (HEM).

When this change comes into effect, existing EPC certificates calculated under RdSAP 10 will remain valid for their 10-year lifespan. However, any new assessments will use HEM, which may score the same property differently.

For landlords currently close to the C boundary on an older certificate, this methodology change is worth understanding. Properties assessed under the new model may score slightly lower, making the jump to C harder than it currently appears.

The practical implication: If your property is rated D and you get a new assessment under HEM, it may come back as E. Getting a survey now — before the methodology changes — means you're working with current rules. It also means improvements you make now may benefit from more favourable scoring.
